→ The Power Questions — Deep Dive
WHAT?
Define the problem clearly — strip away the noise
Before solving anything, make sure you actually understand what's in front of you.
20 questions
Granular Questions to Ask
→ What exactly is happening here?
→ What are the facts vs. opinions?
→ What am I actually being told?
→ What is the core claim or argument?
→ What kind of claim is it — fact, forecast, value judgement or definition?
→ What evidence supports this?
→ What evidence is missing?
→ What is NOT being said here?
→ What assumptions am I making?
→ What are the key terms & do I truly understand them?
→ What's the scope — how big is this really?
→ What do I know for certain, and what am I only guessing?
→ What would I expect to see if this were true? And if it were false?
→ What is the simplest explanation that fits all the facts?
→ What is signal here, and what is noise?
→ What is the question behind the question?
→ What problem am I really solving — the symptom or the cause?
→ What exactly is the decision I have to take, if any?
→ What does "success" look like, in measurable terms?
→ What would change my mind about this?

WHEN?

WHICH?
Compare the options — every choice is a trade-off
A choice between one option is not a choice. Put the alternatives side by side.
12 questions
Granular Questions to Ask
→ Which options have I not even listed yet?
→ Which criteria actually matter here, and in what order?
→ Which option would I pick if my favourite were ruled out?
→ Which trade-off am I making — and am I making it knowingly?
→ Which of these facts, if wrong, would flip my decision?
→ Which assumption is carrying the most weight?
→ Which evidence is strongest, and which is merely loudest?
→ Which explanation predicts things the others cannot?
→ Which source is closest to the original event?
→ Which parts are reversible, and which are one-way?
→ Which option keeps the most doors open?
→ Which mistake is cheaper — acting wrongly or waiting wrongly?
HOW MUCH?
Put a number on it — vague words hide weak thinking
"Significant", "many", "risky" — ask for the figure, the unit and the denominator.
12 questions
Granular Questions to Ask
→ How much, exactly? In what unit?
→ How many out of how many? What is the denominator?
→ How big is the effect in absolute terms, not only as a percentage?
→ How large was the sample, and is that enough?
→ How wide is the range of plausible values, not just the average?
→ How often does this kind of thing happen in general?
→ How much of the change is just normal ups and downs?
→ How much better is this than the next best option?
→ How sure am I — 60%, 90%, 99%? What would shift that number?
→ How much would it cost to be wrong? And to be late?
→ How much evidence would a neutral person ask for?
→ How much time does this decision actually deserve?
COMPARED TO WHAT?
Find the baseline — nothing is big or small on its own
A number without a comparison is only decoration. Always ask for the baseline.

Granular Questions to Ask

→ What is the base rate for cases like this?
→ What happened in the control group?
→ What would have happened anyway, without this intervention?
→ Is this unusual, or does it only feel unusual because I just noticed it?
→ How do similar projects, people or companies usually fare?
→ Is the comparison fair — like with like?
→ What is the benchmark, and who chose it?
→ Is this risk larger than risks I already accept every day?
→ What does the full distribution look like, not only the winner?
→ What is the opportunity cost — what else could this time or money do?
→ Compared to my own earlier prediction, how did reality turn out?
WHAT ELSE?

Granular Questions to Ask

→ What else changed at the same time?
→ Could it be coincidence? Could the cause run the other way?
→ Is a hidden third factor driving both things?
→ What else should I have seen by now if my theory is right?
→ What am I not seeing because I am not looking for it?
→ What else did the same source say that I can check?
→ What other fields have solved a problem shaped like this?
→ What would a complete outsider notice first?
→ What else could I do with the same resources?
→ What if both sides are partly right — or both wrong?

→ The Socratic Six — Questions That Open Up Any Conversation

Six kinds of question, used in order or alone. Ask them of others gently, and of yourself without mercy.

CLARIFY
Questions of clarification
Most disagreements shrink once both sides mean the same thing by the same words.
7 questions
Questions to Ask
→ What exactly do you mean by that?
→ Can you put it another way?
→ Can you give me a concrete example?
→ What is the main point, in one sentence?
→ How does this connect to what we were discussing?
→ Is that the same as saying X, or something different?
→ What would be a clear case of this — and a clear non-case?
ASSUMPTIONS
Questions that probe assumptions
Every argument stands on things it does not say aloud.
7 questions
Questions to Ask
→ What are we taking for granted here?
→ Is that always the case, or only sometimes?
→ What would happen if that assumption were false?
→ Why would someone make this assumption?
→ How could we check whether the assumption holds?
→ What is being assumed about people's motives?
→ Which of these assumptions did I inherit without ever examining?
EVIDENCE

→ Is that evidence enough? What would be enough?
→ Could the same evidence support a different conclusion?
→ Is there any reason to doubt this evidence?
→ What would count as evidence against it?
→ Is this a typical example or an exception?
→ What led you to believe this in the first place?

CONSEQUENCES

→ Does this fit with what I said earlier, or contradict it?

→ Is this a principle I would accept if it were applied to me?

→ Take Thinking Apart, Then Grade It — The Paul–Elder Model

The most widely taught model of critical thinking: eight parts that every piece of reasoning has, nine standards to judge them by, and eight habits of mind that keep the whole thing honest.

◆ The 8 Elements of Thought — Take Any Reasoning Apart 8

Every piece of reasoning — yours or anyone's — has these eight parts. Name them and the weak one shows itself.

Purpose
All reasoning is aimed at some goal.
→ What am I trying to accomplish?
→ Is this purpose realistic and fair?
→ Have I drifted away from it?
Question at Issue
All reasoning tries to settle some question.
→ What precise question am I answering?
→ Is it one question, or several tangled together?
→ Is it a matter of fact, preference or judgement?
Information
Data, facts, observations and experience.
→ What information am I using?
→ Is it accurate, relevant and sufficient?
→ What do I still need to find out?
Inference & Interpretation
The conclusions drawn from the information.
→ How did I get from the data to this conclusion?
→ Is there another reasonable interpretation?
→ Does the conclusion go beyond the evidence?
Concepts
The ideas, theories and definitions doing the work.
→ What main idea am I using to make sense of this?
→ Am I using this word the way others use it?
→ Would a different concept explain it better?
Assumptions
What is taken for granted without being stated.
→ What am I taking for granted?
→ Is that justified?
→ Which assumption would hurt the most if it were wrong?
Implications & Consequences
Where the reasoning leads if you follow it.
→ If I accept this, what follows?
→ What are the consequences, intended and unintended?
→ Am I willing to accept them?
Point of View
The position from which the matter is being seen.
→ From what position am I looking at this?
→ What does this vantage point hide?
→ Whose view have I not considered?

◆ The 9 Intellectual Standards — Grade the Quality of Thinking 9

Run any statement, report or argument through these nine tests.

Clarity
Can it be understood at all?
→ Could I state it plainly, with an example?
→ Would a stranger understand what I mean?
Accuracy
Is it true?
→ How could this be checked?
→ Has it been checked — by whom?
Precision
Is it specific enough?
→ Can I be more exact — how much, how many, when?
→ Is a vague word hiding a missing detail?
Relevance
Does it bear on the question?
→ How does this connect to the issue?
→ Does it help, or does it merely sound good?
Depth
Does it deal with the hard parts?
→ What makes this problem difficult?
→ Which complexities am I skipping over?
Breadth
Does it consider other angles?
→ Do I need another perspective?
→ What would the other side add?
Logic
Does it hang together?
→ Does all of this fit together?
→ Does the conclusion really follow from the reasons?
Significance
Is it the important thing?
→ Is this the most important issue to consider?
→ Which of these facts matters most?
Fairness
Is it free of self-interest?
→ Am I representing other views honestly?
→ Would I accept this reasoning if it cut against me?

◆ The 8 Intellectual Traits — Check the Thinker 8

Skills are not enough. These are the habits of character that make the skills honest.

Intellectual Humility
Knowing the limits of what you know.
→ Where does my knowledge end on this subject?
Intellectual Courage
Facing ideas you would rather avoid.
→ Which idea am I avoiding because it is uncomfortable?
Intellectual Empathy
Entering another's view accurately.
→ Can I state their view so well that they would accept my summary?
Intellectual Autonomy
Thinking it through for yourself.
→ Do I believe this because I reasoned it out, or because my group does?
Intellectual Integrity
One standard for yourself and for others.
→ Am I holding myself to the standard I demand of others?
Intellectual Perseverance
Staying with a difficult problem.
→ Am I giving up because it is wrong, or only because it is hard?
Confidence in Reason
Trusting evidence and argument over pressure.
→ Am I persuading with reasons, or with force and volume?
Fair-Mindedness
Treating all viewpoints by the same rules.
→ Am I favouring this view because it benefits me?
→ Field Kits — 18 Checklists For Real Situations

Pick the card that matches what is in front of you. You do not need every question — the two or three that sting are the ones to answer.

SOURCE
Before you believe a source
The SIFT moves and lateral reading: leave the page and see what others say about it.
12 questions
Checklist
→ Stop: do I know this source? What is my first emotional reaction?
→ What do independent sources say about this site, author or organisation?
→ What is their expertise — and is it in THIS subject?
→ How do they make money, and who funds them?
→ Is this the original source, or a copy of a copy?
→ Can I find better coverage of the same claim elsewhere?
→ Do they cite evidence I can open and read for myself?
→ Do they correct their mistakes openly?
→ Does the headline match what the article actually says?
→ Is the quote, statistic or image shown in its original context?
→ What is the date — is something old being recirculated as new?
→ Would I trust this source if it were saying the opposite?
NEWS FEED
Before you react or share
Outrage, fear and smugness travel fastest. Feeling strongly is the signal to slow down.
13 questions
Checklist
→ Is this making me angry, afraid or smug — and is that the point?
→ Who gains if I share this in the next ten seconds?
→ Is it reporting, opinion, advertisement or satire?
→ Does it use emotional language where facts should be?
→ Does it offer only two choices when there are more?
→ Does it blame one person or group for a complicated problem?
→ Does it attack the person instead of the argument?
→ Does it contradict itself from one line to the next?
→ Why did the algorithm show me this — and what is it not showing me?
→ Have I read past the headline?
→ Is it widely shared because it is true, or because it is shareable?
→ If this were about "my side", would I judge it the same way?
→ Is this worth my attention at all, or is ignoring it the wise move?
NUMBERS
Before you trust a statistic
Numbers look objective. Somebody still chose what to count, how, and what to show you.
14 questions
Checklist
→ What exactly was counted, and how was it defined?
→ Out of how many? What is the denominator?
→ Is this a relative risk or an absolute risk?
→ Mean or median — and would the other tell a different story?
→ How big was the sample, and how was it selected?
→ What is the margin of error, or the range?
→ Is the chart's axis cut, stretched, or starting at a convenient date?
→ Is it a big percentage of a very small number?
→ Could the combined figure hide opposite trends inside the subgroups?
→ Is this extreme result likely to drift back towards average by itself?
→ Were many things measured and only the striking one reported?
→ Is "statistically significant" being confused with "large" or "important"?
→ Who produced the number, and what did they want it to show?
→ Does it pass a rough sanity check against what I already know?
STUDY
"A study shows…" — before you repeat it
One study is one data point. Ask what kind, how big, and whether anyone has repeated it.
14 questions
Checklist
→ Is it an experiment, or only an observation?
→ Was there a control group, and was assignment random?
→ Humans, animals or cells in a dish?
→ How many participants, and for how long?
→ Was the outcome that matters measured, or a stand-in for it?
→ Was the plan registered before the data came in?
→ Has anyone independently replicated it?
→ Is this one study, or the weight of many?
→ Peer-reviewed paper, preprint or press release?
→ Who funded it, and do the authors declare conflicts of interest?
→ How large is the effect, and how wide is the uncertainty?
→ Do the authors' own stated limitations undo the headline?
→ Does the news report match what the paper actually claims?
→ Who was studied — and do they resemble the people I care about?
CAUSE
Before you say "X causes Y"
Two things moving together is where the investigation starts, not where it ends.
12 questions
Checklist
→ Did the cause come before the effect?
→ Could the effect be causing the "cause"?
→ Is a third factor driving both?
→ Could it be coincidence — how many pairs were examined to find this one?
→ Is there a believable mechanism?
→ Does more of the cause bring more of the effect?
→ Does the link hold in different places, groups and times?
→ What happens when the cause is removed?
→ Were the groups comparable to begin with?
→ Am I looking only at the survivors or the successes?
→ Is this the single cause, or one contributor among many?
→ Would things have improved anyway, simply with time?
AI ANSWER
Before you rely on an AI answer
Fluent is not the same as correct, and agreeable is not the same as right.
14 questions
Checklist
→ Did I think about this myself first, before asking?
→ Can I verify the key facts from a primary source?
→ Do the citations exist, and do they say what is claimed?
→ Does it sound confident because it is right, or only because it is fluent?
→ Is it simply agreeing with the way I framed the question?
→ What happens if I ask it to argue the opposite?
→ What would a subject expert find wrong here?
→ Is the information current, or frozen at some earlier date?
→ What did it leave out — and did I ask?
→ Did it actually perform the calculation, or only state a result?
→ Do I understand this well enough to defend it without the tool?
→ Am I using it to think better — or to avoid thinking?
→ If this is wrong, who bears the cost — and have I checked in proportion to that?
→ Would I accept this from a human stranger without checking?
PHOTO & VIDEO
Before you trust your eyes and ears
Seeing is no longer believing. Context and origin matter more than how real it looks.
10 questions
Checklist
→ Where did this first appear, and who posted it?
→ Does a reverse image search show an earlier or different context?
→ Are the date, place and event what the caption claims?
→ Has it been cropped, clipped or shortened so that context is lost?
→ Does what comes before and after the clip change its meaning?
→ Do reliable outlets confirm the event independently?
→ Could this be synthetic — and is any provenance information attached?
→ Am I believing it because it is vivid?
→ Am I dismissing a real recording as "fake" because it is inconvenient?
→ Is one image being asked to prove a general claim?
ARGUMENT
Before you accept an argument
Separate the conclusion from the reasons, then test the link between them.
12 questions
Checklist
→ What is the conclusion, stated plainly?
→ What are the stated reasons — and the unstated ones?
→ If the reasons were all true, would the conclusion have to follow?
→ Are the reasons themselves true? How do I know?
→ Is any key word shifting its meaning midway?
→ Is it an argument, or only an assertion repeated with force?
→ Does it lean on an example, an analogy, an authority or data — and how good is each?
→ Where does the analogy break down?
→ What is the strongest counter-argument, and has it been answered?
→ Is the conclusion stronger than the evidence permits ("always", "never", "proves")?
→ Who carries the burden of proof here?
→ Would I accept the same logic if it led to a conclusion I dislike?
EXPERT
Before you defer — or refuse to defer
Neither "an expert said so" nor "experts are always wrong" is thinking.
11 questions
Checklist
→ Is this person an expert in this exact field?
→ Is this their personal view or the considered view of the field?
→ What do most qualified experts say, and how strong is the agreement?
→ Where precisely do the experts disagree, and why?
→ What evidence does the consensus rest upon?
→ Does the expert have a stake in the answer?
→ What is their record on earlier predictions?
→ Do they state their uncertainty honestly?
→ Am I rejecting expertise because I dislike the conclusion?
→ Am I accepting it only because of the title, the confidence or the fame?
→ What would I need to learn before my disagreement is worth anything?
DECISION
Before a decision that matters
A good process will not guarantee a good outcome. It only improves the odds — which is all anyone gets.
14 questions
Checklist
→ What am I actually deciding, and by when must it be decided?
→ Is it reversible? If yes, why am I agonising? If no, why am I rushing?
→ What are at least three real options, including "do nothing"?
→ What would I advise my closest friend to do?
→ How have similar decisions usually turned out for others?
→ Imagine it failed badly — what was the most likely reason?
→ What would have to be true for each option to be the best one?
→ What am I giving up by choosing this?
→ How will I feel about this in ten minutes, ten months and ten years?
→ Am I continuing only because of what I have already spent?
→ Which emotion is driving this, and will it still be there tomorrow?
→ What information would change my choice — and can I get it cheaply?
→ Who disagrees, and have I really heard them out?
→ What is my tripwire — the signal that tells me to stop and rethink?
FORECAST
Before you predict — or believe a prediction
Vague forecasts can never be wrong, which is exactly why they are worthless.
11 questions
Checklist

→ Thinking Frameworks To Apply Daily

🔍 First Principles

  • Identify your assumption — what do you "just know"?
  • Break it down to fundamental truths that cannot be reduced further
  • Question each piece — is this actually true or just widely believed?
  • Rebuild your understanding from the ground up
  • Arrive at your OWN conclusion, not the borrowed one

🎭 Steel Man Method

  • Find the STRONGEST version of the opposing argument
  • Argue FOR the other side as if it's your own position
  • Identify what's genuinely valid in that perspective
  • Only then build your counter — now it's actually strong
  • If you can't steel man it, you don't truly understand it

🔄 Second-Order Thinking

  • First-order: "What happens next?" (everyone does this)
  • Second-order: "And then what happens after that?"
  • Map the chain of consequences — 2nd, 3rd, 4th order effects
  • Check: Who else will react? How will the system adapt?
  • The best decisions consider what most people overlook

🧨 Inversion & Pre-Mortem

  • Instead of "how do I succeed?", ask "what would guarantee failure?"
  • Imagine it is a year later and the plan has failed completely
  • Each person independently writes down why it failed
  • Pool the reasons; rank them by likelihood and by damage
  • Fix the plan against the top reasons — then set tripwires

📊 Outside View (Reference Class)

  • Resist the urge to treat your case as unique
  • Find a class of similar past cases — projects, people, bets
  • Look up how they actually turned out: time, cost, success rate
  • Start your estimate from that base rate
  • Only then adjust — modestly — for what is truly different here

⚖️ Bayesian Updating

  • Start with a prior: how likely was this before the new evidence?
  • Ask: how much more likely is this evidence if the claim is true than if it is false?
  • Move in proportion — strong evidence, big move; weak evidence, small move
  • Never go to 0% or 100% — leave room to update again
  • Repeat with each new fact. Belief is a dial, not a switch

🪜 Five Whys — and Its Limits

  • State the problem as an observable fact
  • Ask "why did that happen?" — and answer with evidence, not a guess
  • Repeat on each answer until you reach something you can change
  • Look for several branches — real problems seldom have a single root
  • Aim at the system that allowed the error, not the person who made it

🕵️ Competing Hypotheses

  • List every plausible explanation, including the unwelcome ones
  • List the evidence and the arguments, for and against
  • Test each item against each hypothesis: fits, does not fit, or neutral?
  • Prize the evidence that discriminates — fits one story but not the others
  • Try to disprove, not to prove; keep the hypothesis with the least against it

🔎 SIFT — The Four Moves

  • Stop — notice your reaction, and what you actually know about the source
  • Investigate the source — who is behind it? Check outside the site itself
  • Find better coverage — what do other reliable sources report?
  • Trace claims, quotes and media back to their original context
  • Spend effort in proportion to how much the claim matters to you

🧑‍🏫 Feynman Technique

  • Write the name of the concept at the top of a blank page
  • Explain it in plain words, as if to a 12-year-old
  • Notice where you get stuck or reach for jargon — that is the gap
  • Go back to the source and repair the gap
  • Simplify again, and add an analogy of your own

🧗 Ladder of Inference

  • Observable data: what was actually said or seen?
  • Selected data: which part did I notice — and which did I skip?
  • Added meaning: what story and assumptions did I attach?
  • Conclusion and belief: what did I decide it means?
  • Before acting, climb back down and test each rung with the other person

🧪 Falsification Test

  • State the claim clearly enough that it could turn out to be wrong
  • Ask: what observation would prove it false?
  • If nothing could ever count against it, it is not telling you much
  • Go looking for the disconfirming case — not for more supporting ones
  • A claim that survives honest attempts to break it has earned some trust

🎁 WRAP Decision Process

  • Widen your options — escape the "whether or not" framing
  • Reality-test your assumptions — seek disconfirming evidence, run small trials
  • Attain distance before deciding — what would you tell a friend?
  • Prepare to be wrong — set tripwires and plan for both outcomes
  • A sound process beats a strong hunch

🧼 Decision Hygiene

  • Aim for accuracy, not self-expression
  • Break the judgement into separate, independent components
  • Judge each component on its facts before forming an overall view
  • Collect opinions independently — before people influence one another
  • Hold back your intuition till the end; then let it speak, once

✂️ The Razors

  • Occam: among explanations that fit, prefer the one with the fewest assumptions
  • Hanlon: do not assume malice where a mistake or a muddle would explain it
  • Hitchens: what is asserted without evidence may be set aside without evidence
  • Sagan: extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence
  • Razors tell you where to look first — they are not proofs

🚧 Chesterton's Fence

  • Before removing a rule, process or tradition, find out why it exists
  • Ask who put it there and what problem they were facing
  • Check whether that problem still exists today
  • "I can't see the reason" means look harder — not "there is no reason"
  • Then keep it, change it or remove it — knowingly

🧭 Scout Mindset

  • A soldier asks "can I believe this?" A scout asks "is it true?"
  • Notice the moment you start defending instead of exploring
  • Hold welcome and unwelcome evidence to the same standard
  • Wear your identity lightly — take pride in updating, not in having been right
  • Treat being wrong as information, not humiliation

🤝 Rapoport's Rules

  • Restate the other position so well that they wish they had put it that way
  • List the points on which you agree
  • Mention anything you have learnt from them
  • Only then offer your rebuttal or criticism
  • Criticise the idea, never the person

🎲 Expected Value & Ruin

  • List all the possible outcomes, not only the likely one
  • Put a rough probability and pay-off on each
  • Multiply and compare — a small chance of a huge loss can dominate
  • Check for ruin: avoid bets you cannot survive losing, whatever the odds
  • Judge the decision by the odds you had, not by how the dice fell

🧱 Toulmin Model

  • Claim: what am I being asked to accept?
  • Grounds: what facts or data support it?
  • Warrant: what principle links those grounds to the claim?
  • Backing: what supports that principle itself?
  • Qualifier and rebuttal: how sure — and under what conditions does it fail?

⭕ Circle of Competence

  • Draw the boundary of what you truly understand
  • Be honest about which side of the line this question falls on
  • Inside: decide. At the edge: slow down. Outside: borrow expertise
  • Widen the circle deliberately, through study and feedback
  • Knowing where the edge is matters more than the size of the circle

🧮 Fermi Estimation

  • Break an impossible-looking number into smaller, guessable parts
  • Estimate each part roughly, with a low and a high bound
  • Multiply through — the errors partly cancel one another
  • Compare the result with any known figure, as a sanity check
  • Use it to catch claims that are wrong by ten times or more

🚫 Critical Ignoring

  • Attention is limited — decide what does NOT deserve it
  • Self-nudge: remove temptations and mute low-quality sources
  • Read laterally: check a source elsewhere before investing time in it
  • Do not feed the trolls: deny bad-faith actors your attention and replies
  • Save deep thinking for sources that have earned it
→ Biases That Hijack Your Thinking — 167 Of Them, Each With Its Antidote Question

The first four groups follow the four problems of the Cognitive Bias Codex — too much information, not enough meaning, the need to act fast, and what to remember. Two more groups cover crowds and the AI age. Do not try to memorise them: learn the question under each one.

⚡ Problem 1 — Too Much Information 28

The brain filters hard. What gets through is what is repeated, vivid, recent, or already believed.

Availability Heuristic
What comes to mind easily feels more true or common than it actually is.
→ Is this truly frequent, or just easy to recall?
Attentional Bias
What you are already preoccupied with is what you keep noticing.
→ What am I missing because I am fixed on this?
Frequency Illusion
Once you notice something, it suddenly seems to be everywhere.
→ Has it increased, or has only my attention increased?
Illusory Truth Effect
Repetition makes a statement feel true — even when you know better.
→ Do I believe this from evidence, or from having heard it often?
Mere-Exposure Effect
Familiar things feel safer and better simply because they are familiar.
→ Do I prefer this on merit, or only on familiarity?
Negativity Bias
Bad news, criticism and threats weigh more than good things of the same size.
→ Am I giving the positive evidence equal weight?
Salience Bias
The vivid, unusual or emotional detail crowds out the dull but important one.
→ Which boring fact matters more than this striking one?
Anchoring Effect
The first piece of info you get becomes the anchor for all your judgements.
→ What would I estimate if I had never seen that first figure?
Framing Effect
The same facts lead to different choices depending on how they are worded.
→ Would I decide the same if it were framed the opposite way?
Contrast Effect
Things look better or worse depending on what they are placed beside.
→ How would I rate this entirely on its own?
Distinction Bias
Side-by-side comparison exaggerates differences that will not matter in real use.
→ Will I notice this difference once I am living with only one?
Focusing Illusion
Whatever you are concentrating on seems more important than it is.
→ How much will this matter when I am no longer thinking about it?
Confirmation Bias
You seek info that proves what you already believe and ignore what doesn't.
→ What have I searched for that could prove me wrong?
Myside Bias
You weigh evidence and arguments in favour of your own side. Intelligence does not cure it.
→ Would I accept this evidence if it supported the other side?
Congruence Bias
You test only your favourite hypothesis and never the rivals.
→ Which alternative explanation have I actually tested?
Selective Perception
Your expectations shape what you perceive in the first place.
→ What would someone with the opposite expectation see here?
Observer-Expectancy Effect
Expecting a result subtly nudges how you gather and read the data.
→ Could someone who does not know my expectation check this?
Ostrich Effect
You avoid information that might be unpleasant.
→ What am I avoiding looking at?
Semmelweis Reflex
New evidence is rejected by reflex because it contradicts established practice.
→ Am I rejecting this because it is wrong, or because it is new?
Conservatism Bias
You update too little when new evidence arrives.
→ Has my confidence moved as much as the evidence warrants?
Continued Influence Effect
A corrected falsehood keeps shaping your thinking after the correction.
→ Which "facts" in my head have since been withdrawn?
Choice-Supportive Bias
After choosing, you remember your option as better than it was.
→ Am I defending this because it is good, or because I chose it?
Bias Blind Spot
You spot biases in others far more easily than in yourself.
→ Which bias would my critic say I am showing right now?
Naïve Realism
You feel you see the world as it is, so those who disagree must be ignorant or biased.
→ What if they are seeing something real that I cannot?
Empathy Gap
In a calm state you underestimate how emotion will drive you later — and the reverse.
→ How would I judge this in the opposite mood?
Subjective Validation
A statement feels accurate because it holds personal meaning for you.
→ Would this description fit almost anyone?
Base Rate Neglect
You ignore how common something is in general and focus on the particular case.
→ How often does this happen overall, before the details?
Scope Neglect
Your concern does not scale with size — 2,000 and 200,000 feel much the same.
→ Have I actually looked at the magnitude?

⚡ Problem 2 — Not Enough Meaning 46

Faced with gaps, the mind supplies patterns, stories, stereotypes and motives of its own.

Clustering Illusion
You see meaningful clusters and streaks in random data.
→ Would pure chance produce a pattern like this fairly often?
Apophenia & Pareidolia
You perceive connections, faces and intentions in noise.
→ What is the evidence beyond "it looks like"?
Illusory Correlation
You believe two things are linked because a few memorable cases coincided.
→ Have I counted the times they did NOT go together?
Law of Small Numbers
You draw firm conclusions from samples that are far too small.
→ How many cases is this really based on?
Gambler's Fallacy
You expect independent random events to "even out" in the short run.
→ Does the coin remember the previous tosses?
Hot-Hand Belief
You expect a streak to continue.
→ Is there a real mechanism behind the streak, or only a run of luck?
Evidence note Long taught as a pure fallacy. A corrected analysis of the original data found the hot hand can be real in basketball. Judge case by case.
Regression Fallacy
You credit or blame an action for what was only a natural return to average.
→ Would it have drifted back to normal anyway?
Conjunction Fallacy
A detailed, specific story feels more probable than a simple general one.
→ Can two conditions together be likelier than one alone?
Neglect of Probability
You react to the mere possibility of an outcome and ignore how unlikely it is.
→ What is the actual chance, as a number?
Narrative Fallacy
You stitch scattered facts into a neat causal story, and then believe the story.
→ Which facts did I leave out to make the story flow?
Proportionality Bias
Big events feel as if they must have big causes.
→ Could something small or accidental have produced this?
Illusion of Validity
A coherent picture makes you overconfident in a prediction.
→ How well have predictions of this kind done in the past?
Survivorship Bias
You only see the winners. The graveyard of failures is invisible to you.
→ Where are the ones who did the same thing and failed?
Hindsight Bias
After the event, it feels as if it was obvious all along.
→ What did I actually predict beforehand — in writing?
Outcome Bias
You judge a decision by how it turned out, not by how sound it was.
→ Given what was known then, was it a good bet?
Fundamental Attribution Error
You explain others' behaviour by their character, and your own by circumstances.
→ What situation would make a reasonable person act like this?
Actor–Observer Bias
The same act is "the situation" when you do it and "their personality" when they do.
→ Would I excuse myself for the same thing?
Just-World Hypothesis
You assume people get what they deserve.
→ Am I blaming the victim to keep my world tidy?
Hostile Attribution Bias
You read ambiguous behaviour as deliberately hostile.
→ What is the innocent explanation?
Stereotyping
You fill the gaps about a person with assumptions about their group.
→ What do I actually know about this individual?
Group Attribution Error
You treat one member as representing the whole group.
→ Is one example standing in for thousands of people?
Essentialism
You treat categories of people or things as having a fixed inner nature.
→ Is this difference inherent, or produced by circumstance?
Halo & Horn Effect
One good or bad trait colours your judgement of everything else about a person.
→ Am I rating the idea, or the person's charm, looks or fame?
Authority Bias
Someone important said it, so it must be true. Titles don't equal truth.
→ Would the argument hold if an unknown person made it?
Bandwagon Effect
Everyone believes it, so it must be right. Popular ≠ correct.
→ What is the evidence, apart from the number of believers?
In-Group Bias
You favour, trust and forgive your own group more readily.
→ Would I judge this the same if the other group did it?
Out-Group Homogeneity
"They" all seem alike; "we" are varied individuals.
→ How much variety exists inside that group?
Not-Invented-Here
You discount ideas because they came from outside.
→ If we had thought of this ourselves, would we like it?
Reactive Devaluation
A proposal seems worse simply because the other side made it.
→ How would I rate this offer if my own side had made it?
Mental Accounting
You treat money differently depending on the label you have put on it.
→ Is this money any different from the rest of my money?
Normalcy Bias
You assume things will carry on as always, and underprepare for disruption.
→ What is my plan if this time is not like the others?
Zero-Sum Bias
You assume one side's gain must be the other side's loss.
→ Is there an outcome in which both sides gain?
Curse of Knowledge
Once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it.
→ Would a newcomer follow this explanation?
Illusion of Transparency
You overestimate how well others can read your thoughts and feelings.
→ Have I actually said this aloud, clearly?
Spotlight Effect
You overestimate how much other people notice you.
→ How much do I notice other people's slips?
Illusion of Asymmetric Insight
You believe you understand others better than they understand you.
→ What might they understand about me that I do not?
Projection Bias
You assume your future self, and other people, share your present tastes and feelings.
→ Will I still want this when my mood or situation changes?
Planning Fallacy
You underestimate the time, cost and risk of your own plans.
→ How long did similar work actually take last time?
Impact Bias
You overestimate how strongly, and for how long, an event will affect your feelings.
→ How did I feel a few months after the last "huge" event?
Pro-Innovation Bias
You overrate a new thing's usefulness and underrate its limits.
→ What does this do worse than the thing it replaces?
Declinism & Rosy Retrospection
The past looks better than it was; the present looks worse.
→ What does the data say, as against my nostalgia?
Pessimism Bias
You overestimate the chance that things will go badly.
→ What is the realistic range, not the worst imaginable?
Anthropomorphism
You attribute human intentions to animals, objects, markets and machines.
→ Does it really "want" anything, or am I supplying the motive?
Illusion of Explanatory Depth
You feel you understand how something works — until you try to explain it.
→ Can I explain it step by step, without hand-waving?
Recency Illusion
You assume something is new because you have only recently noticed it.
→ How long has this actually been around?
Confabulation
You sincerely invent reasons for your own choices after the fact.
→ Is this my real reason, or a story I assembled later?

⚡ Problem 3 — Need to Act Fast 46

To act at all, the mind favours confidence, the present, the familiar, and whatever it has already invested in.

Overconfidence Effect
Your confidence runs ahead of your accuracy.
→ When I felt this sure before, how often was I right?
Dunning–Kruger Effect
Self-assessment is a poor guide to competence — at every level of skill.
→ What objective feedback do I have on my skill here?
Evidence note The popular version ("the incompetent are the most confident") is contested. Critics call it a statistical artefact, and a 2026 Psychological Review paper argues that, once corrected, high performers are the most overconfident. Never use it as an insult.
Illusory Superiority
Most people rate themselves above average on most things.
→ What evidence, other than my own opinion, places me above average?
Optimism Bias
You believe bad outcomes are less likely for you than for others.
→ Why would the usual odds not apply to me?
Illusion of Control
You overestimate your influence over events ruled largely by chance.
→ Which part of this outcome do I actually control?
Self-Serving Bias
Successes are your doing; failures are someone else's fault.
→ What part did I play in the failure — and luck in the success?
Egocentric Bias
You overweight your own perspective and your own contribution.
→ How would the others divide the credit?
False Consensus Effect
You overestimate how many people think as you do.
→ Have I actually asked, or am I assuming?
Third-Person Effect
You believe advertising and propaganda work on others, not on you.
→ How might this message be working on me right now?
Barnum (Forer) Effect
Vague, flattering descriptions feel uniquely about you.
→ Would most people say "that's me" to this?
Present Bias
You overvalue now and discount later, far more than you would on reflection.
→ What would my future self ask me to do today?
Affect Heuristic
Your liking or disliking of something stands in for a real assessment of its risks and benefits.
→ If I felt neutral about this, how would I weigh it?
Identifiable Victim Effect
One named person moves you more than a large statistic.
→ Am I responding to the size of the need, or to the story?
Sunk Cost Fallacy
You keep going because you've invested, not because it still makes sense.
→ If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?
Escalation of Commitment
After a bad result, you double down to justify the earlier decision.
→ Am I adding resources to succeed, or to avoid admitting an error?
Plan Continuation Bias
You stick to the original plan even as the conditions change.
→ What has changed since the plan was made?
Commitment & Consistency
Having said or done something, you bend later choices to look consistent.
→ Am I being consistent with the facts, or only with my past self?
Loss Aversion
A loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases.
→ Would I pay this much to acquire what I am afraid of losing?
Endowment Effect
You value a thing more simply because it is yours.
→ What would I pay for this if I did not already own it?
Disposition Effect
You sell winners too early and hold on to losers too long.
→ If I held cash today, would I buy this at today's price?
IKEA Effect & Effort Justification
You overvalue what you built, or suffered for.
→ Would a stranger rate my work as highly as I do?
Status Quo Bias
The current state feels right simply because it is current.
→ If the alternative were the default, would I switch back to this?
Default Effect
You accept whichever option is pre-selected.
→ Who chose this default, and in whose interest?
System Justification
You defend existing arrangements as fair and natural, because they exist.
→ Would I design it this way if I were starting from nothing?
Omission Bias
Harm from doing nothing feels more forgivable than harm from acting.
→ What is the cost of not acting?
Action Bias
Under pressure you feel you must do something, even when waiting is better.
→ Is doing nothing the best move here?
Zero-Risk Bias
You prefer removing a small risk entirely over greatly reducing a large one.
→ Which option removes the most risk in total?
Ambiguity Aversion
You avoid options whose odds are unknown, even when they may be better.
→ Am I rejecting this because it is bad, or because it is unclear?
Information Bias
You keep gathering information that cannot change the decision.
→ Would any possible answer alter what I do?
Decoy Effect
A deliberately inferior third option pushes you towards a particular choice.
→ Which option is here only to make another look good?
Scarcity Effect
Things seem more valuable when they appear rare or about to run out.
→ Would I want this if there were plenty of it?
Less-Is-Better Effect
Judged alone, a smaller but "complete" option can look better than a larger one.
→ How do the options compare when placed side by side?
Reactance
Told that you cannot or must, you want the opposite.
→ Am I choosing this on merit, or just to resist being told?
Belief Bias
You judge an argument's logic by whether you like its conclusion.
→ Does the conclusion follow, whether or not I agree with it?
Rhyme-as-Reason Effect
Statements that are catchy, rhyming or easy to read feel more true.
→ Stripped of its phrasing, is the claim supported?
Bikeshedding
Groups spend the most time on trivial matters that everyone understands.
→ Is the time we are spending in proportion to the stakes?
Law of the Instrument
With a hammer in hand, everything looks like a nail.
→ Am I choosing the tool for the problem, or the problem for the tool?
Einstellung Effect
A familiar solution blocks you from seeing a better one.
→ How would I solve this if my usual method were forbidden?
Functional Fixedness
You see objects and people only in their customary role.
→ What else could this be used for?
Streetlight Effect
You search where it is easy to look, not where the answer is likely to be.
→ Am I measuring what matters, or what is convenient?
Premature Closure
You stop thinking once a plausible answer appears.
→ What else could it be — and have I ruled it out?
Risk Compensation
Feeling safer, you take more risk.
→ Has the safety measure changed how carefully I behave?
Moral Licensing
Having done something good, you permit yourself something less good.
→ Am I using a past good deed as a permit?
Normalisation of Deviance
Small rule-breaks that cause no harm slowly become the accepted standard.
→ Would this have been acceptable to us a year ago?
Social Desirability Bias
People report what sounds good rather than what is true.
→ Would this answer change if it were anonymous?
Backfire Effect
The claim that correcting a falsehood makes people believe it more strongly.
→ Am I holding back a correction out of a fear that is mostly unfounded?
Evidence note Mostly a myth. A replication with over 10,000 participants and 52 issues found almost no backfire; corrections generally do move factual beliefs.

⚡ Problem 4 — What Should We Remember? 18

Memory is rebuilt each time it is recalled. It keeps the gist, the peaks, and whatever came last.

Misinformation Effect
Information received after an event alters your memory of the event.
→ What did I record at the time, before others gave their version?
Source Confusion
You remember the content but forget where it came from.
→ Where exactly did I learn this — and was that source reliable?
Sleeper Effect
A message from a doubtful source grows more persuasive as you forget the source.
→ Did I once have a reason to distrust this?
Cryptomnesia
A forgotten memory returns as if it were your own original idea.
→ Could I have read or heard this somewhere?
False Memory & Suggestibility
Leading questions and repetition can create memories of things that never happened.
→ Is there any independent record of this?
Flashbulb Memory Illusion
Vivid, emotional memories feel exact, but drift like any other.
→ Am I confusing vividness with accuracy?
Consistency Bias
You remember your past attitudes as closer to your present ones than they were.
→ What did I actually write or say back then?
Peak–End Rule
You judge an experience by its most intense moment and by its ending.
→ What was the whole experience like, on average?
Duration Neglect
How long something lasted barely affects how you remember it.
→ Am I ignoring how long this actually went on?
Primacy & Recency Effects
The first and the last items are remembered best; the middle fades.
→ What was in the middle that I have forgotten?
Recency Bias
The latest events count for too much in your judgement.
→ What does the longer record show?
Levelling & Sharpening
With each retelling, some details drop out and others get exaggerated.
→ How far is this account from the first-hand version?
Telescoping Effect
Distant events feel more recent, and recent ones more distant.
→ What is the actual date?
Fading Affect Bias
The bad feelings attached to memories fade faster than the good ones.
→ Am I forgetting how difficult it really was?
Mood-Congruent Memory
Your present mood decides which memories come to mind.
→ Would I recall different examples in a different mood?
Stereotype-Consistent Memory
You recall the details that fit your expectations of a group, and lose those that do not.
→ Which details that did not fit have I dropped?
Implicit Associations
Automatic links between groups and traits act faster than conscious thought.
→ Would I react the same way if the person's group were different?
Google Effect
You tend to forget what you know you can look up.
→ Do I know this, or only where to find it?

⚡ Beyond the Codex — In Groups & Crowds 16

Belonging is a powerful motive. In company, accuracy often loses to harmony, status and identity.

Groupthink
The wish for harmony suppresses doubt and dissent.
→ Who here disagrees but has not said so?
Conformity
You adjust your stated view — sometimes even what you perceive — to match the group.
→ What would I say if I were answering alone, in writing?
Social Proof
Unsure what to do, you copy what others appear to be doing.
→ Do they know something, or are they copying each other too?
Group Polarisation
Discussion among the like-minded pushes a group to a more extreme position.
→ Did the discussion add evidence, or only intensity?
Pluralistic Ignorance
Everyone privately doubts, but assumes that everyone else agrees.
→ Has anyone actually checked what people privately think?
Abilene Paradox
A group agrees to something that no individual member actually wants.
→ Does anyone here really want this?
Shared Information Bias
Groups discuss what everyone already knows and neglect what only one member knows.
→ What does each person know that the others do not?
Bystander Effect
The more people present, the less each one feels responsible to act.
→ If not me, then who — by name?
Availability Cascade
A claim gains credibility merely by being repeated across public conversation.
→ How many truly independent sources are there?
Identity-Protective Cognition
You reject evidence that threatens the beliefs of the group you belong to.
→ What would it cost me socially to accept this?
Hostile Media Effect
Partisans on both sides see the same neutral coverage as biased against them.
→ Would the other side also call this biased — against them?
Spiral of Silence
People who think they are in the minority keep quiet, so the minority looks smaller still.
→ Which views are missing because people are afraid to voice them?
HiPPO Effect
The highest-paid person's opinion ends the discussion.
→ Would this idea survive if the junior-most person had proposed it?
Courtesy Bias
People give the polite answer rather than the honest one.
→ Have I made it safe to tell me the truth?
Extrinsic Incentives Bias
You assume others are driven by money and reward, and yourself by principle.
→ Might their motives be as mixed as my own?
Naïve Cynicism
You expect more selfish bias in others than is actually there.
→ What evidence do I have of bad faith?

⚡ Beyond the Codex — The Digital & AI Age 13

New tools, old brain. Machines and feeds add their own distortions to the ones we already carry.

Automation Bias
You favour a machine's suggestion over contradicting evidence or your own judgement.
→ Would I accept this from a human colleague without checking?
Automation Complacency
When the system is usually right, you stop monitoring it.
→ When did I last verify its output independently?
AI Sycophancy
Chatbots tend to agree with you and affirm your framing, which feels like confirmation.
→ Have I asked it to argue against me?
Evidence note A 2026 study in Science found that 11 leading AI models affirmed users' actions about 49% more often than humans did. Users came away more convinced they were right.
ELIZA Effect
You attribute understanding, feeling or intention to software that produces fluent text.
→ Am I trusting it because it sounds like a person?
Fluency Heuristic
Smooth, confident, well-formatted text feels more accurate.
→ Is it right, or merely well written?
Cognitive Offloading
Handing the thinking to a tool saves effort now and may weaken the skill later.
→ Have I attempted this myself before asking?
Evidence note In a 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in AI went with less critical thinking; more confidence in oneself went with more.
Algorithm Aversion & Appreciation
You either abandon an algorithm after one visible error, or trust it blindly.
→ What is its actual track record against the human alternative?
Filter Bubble & Echo Chamber
Personalised feeds and self-chosen networks narrow what you get to see.
→ When did I last read a strong source that I disagree with?
Evidence note Researchers still debate how large this effect really is.
Engagement Bias
Platforms promote what provokes reaction, so the outrageous looks more common than it is.
→ Is this representative, or only what travels well?
Popularity Cue
Likes, views and follower counts stand in for credibility.
→ Would I believe this with the numbers hidden?
Search Confirmation
The way you word a search tends to return the answer you were hoping for.
→ What would I find if I searched for the opposite?
Liar's Dividend
Because fakes exist, genuine evidence can be waved away as fake.
→ Am I calling this fake on evidence, or because it is unwelcome?
Truth-Default
You assume what you are told is honest unless something triggers suspicion.
→ What would I check if I assumed nothing?

🎯 Faulty Arguments & Manipulation Tactics — Name It, Then Check It 46

Biases happen inside your head. These happen in the argument put in front of you — sometimes by mistake, sometimes on purpose. Spotting one does not prove the conclusion false; it only means this argument has not earned it.

Ad Hominem
Attacking the person instead of the argument.
→ Is the claim wrong, or do I just dislike who said it?
Straw Man
Refuting a weaker version of what was actually said.
→ Is this what they really claimed?
False Dilemma
Presenting two options when more exist.
→ What third and fourth options are missing?
Middle-Ground Fallacy
Assuming the truth must lie halfway between two positions.
→ Is the midpoint supported by evidence, or only by politeness?
Slippery Slope
Claiming one step must lead to an extreme end, without showing the links.
→ What is the evidence for each step in the chain?
Circular Reasoning
The conclusion is hidden inside the premise.
→ Does the reason assume what it is meant to prove?
Non Sequitur
The conclusion simply does not follow from what came before.
→ What is the missing link, and is it true?
Appeal to Authority
Treating a claim as true because of who said it.
→ What is the evidence, apart from the name?
Appeal to Popularity
Many believe it, therefore it is true.
→ Were the many right about this kind of thing before?
Appeal to Emotion
Fear, pity, pride or outrage in place of reasons.
→ What remains once the emotion is removed?
Appeal to Nature
Natural means good; artificial means bad.
→ Is it safe and effective — whatever its origin?
Appeal to Tradition
It has always been done this way.
→ Is it still the best way, or only the oldest?
Appeal to Novelty
It is new, therefore it is better.
→ Better at what, according to which evidence?
Appeal to Ignorance
Nobody has disproved it, so it must be true.
→ Where is the positive evidence?
Appeal to Consequences
It would be awful if it were true, so it must be false.
→ Does wishing change the facts?
Shifting the Burden of Proof
Demanding that doubters disprove a claim the claimant never supported.
→ Who made the claim, and have they supported it?
Post Hoc
It happened after, so it happened because.
→ Would it have happened anyway, without the earlier event?
Correlation as Causation
Two things move together, so one must cause the other.
→ Chance, reverse cause, or a hidden third factor?
Single-Cause Fallacy
One cause is named for an outcome that has many.
→ What are the other contributors?
Hasty Generalisation
A broad rule drawn from a handful of cases.
→ Is the sample large and representative enough?
Anecdotal Evidence
A vivid personal story set against systematic data.
→ Is this one case, or the pattern?
Cherry-Picking
Showing only the evidence that suits.
→ What does the complete data set show?
Texas Sharpshooter
Painting the target around the bullet holes — finding the pattern after seeing the data.
→ Was the hypothesis stated before or after looking?
No True Scotsman
Redefining the group to exclude every counter-example.
→ Is the definition changing to protect the claim?
Moving the Goalposts
Demanding fresh proof each time the previous demand is met.
→ What standard was agreed at the start?
Special Pleading
Exempting your own case from the rule you apply to others.
→ Why should this case be the exception?
Red Herring
Introducing an irrelevant point to change the subject.
→ Does this answer the question that was asked?
Whataboutism
Deflecting criticism by pointing at someone else's fault.
→ Even if that is true, has the original point been answered?
Equivocation
A key word quietly changes its meaning midway.
→ Does this word mean the same thing in each sentence?
Motte-and-Bailey
Advancing a bold claim, then retreating to a modest one when challenged.
→ Which claim is being defended — the bold one or the safe one?
Loaded Question
A question that smuggles in an unproven assumption.
→ What does the question take for granted?
Composition & Division
What is true of the parts must be true of the whole — or the reverse.
→ Does the property really transfer between part and whole?
Ecological Fallacy
Drawing conclusions about individuals from group averages.
→ Does the average tell me anything about this one person?
Genetic Fallacy
Judging a claim by where it came from rather than on its merits.
→ Is it true, regardless of its origin?
False Equivalence
Treating two things as equal because they share one feature.
→ Are they alike in the ways that matter?
False Balance
Giving two positions equal airtime when the evidence sits heavily on one side.
→ How is the evidence actually distributed?
Nirvana Fallacy
Rejecting a real improvement because it is not perfect.
→ Compared to what realistic alternative?
Proving Too Much
An argument which, if accepted, would also prove things that are plainly absurd.
→ What else would this same logic justify?
Gish Gallop
Burying the other side under more weak points than can be answered in the time.
→ Which single point is the strongest — and does it stand?
Just Asking Questions
Insinuating a claim through questions, to avoid having to defend it.
→ What claim is being implied, and what supports it?
Galileo Gambit
"They laughed at Galileo too" — rejection is taken as proof of being right.
→ Being doubted is common. Where is the evidence?
Scapegoating
Blaming one person or group for a complex problem.
→ What are the actual causes — and who gains from this blame?
Impossible Expectations
Demanding a level of certainty that no evidence could ever meet.
→ Is the same standard applied to the alternative view?
Fake Experts
Presenting unqualified or unrepresentative voices as authorities.
→ What do the relevant specialists, as a body, say?
Conspiracy Reasoning
Every piece of contrary evidence is absorbed as part of the plot.
→ What evidence could, even in principle, count against it?
Fallacy Fallacy
Assuming a conclusion is false because one argument for it was bad.
→ Is there a better argument for the same conclusion?

What Actually Reduces Bias — Process Over Willpower

Knowing the names is not enough. People spot biases in others far more easily than in themselves, and intelligence does not close that gap. What helps is changing how the decision gets made.

01 Consider the opposite. Before settling, write down a couple of reasons your first judgement could be wrong.
02 Take the outside view. Start from how similar cases actually turned out, then adjust for this one.
03 Run a pre-mortem. Assume the plan has already failed. List why. Fix those things first.
04 Decide separately, then discuss. Collect written views before anyone speaks, so that one voice does not anchor the rest.
05 Use a checklist for repeat decisions. Same questions, same order, every time.
06 Create delay. Sleep on anything important, irreversible or emotional.
07 Put numbers on beliefs. Write down a probability. Score yourself later.
08 Keep a decision journal. Record what you knew, expected and felt — before the outcome is known.
09 Appoint a real challenger. Rotate the role, and thank the dissent instead of punishing it.
10 Change the environment. Remove the anchor, hide the names, blind the evaluation.
11 Set tripwires in advance. Define now what will make you stop or reverse later.
12 Ask someone with no stake. Distance does what willpower cannot.

🔬 Science Check — What Newer Research Has Changed

A critical thinker also questions the critical-thinking literature. Several famous findings have been revised, weakened or overturned. Last reviewed: November 2026.

Dunning–Kruger is contested
The popular "Mount Stupid" graph — confidence peaking at zero skill — was never in the 1999 paper. Critics have long argued the real pattern is partly regression to the mean, and a July 2026 paper in Psychological Review found that, once this is corrected, high-ability people were the most overconfident. David Dunning has rejected the artefact critique in the past, so the debate is live. Safe lesson: confidence tells you little about competence, at any level.
The backfire effect is rare
The idea that correcting people makes them dig in deeper spread widely after 2010. A replication with 10,100 participants across 52 issues found it almost never happens. Corrections usually do improve factual beliefs — though preferences may not move.
The hot hand may be real
For three decades it was the textbook example of seeing patterns in randomness. In 2018, economists showed the original analysis carried a subtle statistical bias; once corrected, the same data support a hot hand. The gambler's fallacy, for truly independent events, still stands.
"Willpower is a battery" is shaky
A 23-laboratory replication of ego depletion found an effect close to zero. A later multi-lab study found only a small one. Do not build a theory of your day on it.
Anchoring and framing have held up
In the Many Labs project, 36 research groups repeated 13 classic findings; 10 replicated, including anchoring and gain-versus-loss framing. Anchoring came out stronger than in the original study.
Being clever does not protect you
The bias blind spot is not reduced by cognitive ability, and repeated statements gain belief even among people who know the correct answer. Rely on process and on other people, not on being smart.
Puzzles do not make you a better thinker in general
A major 2016 review found brain-training improves the trained task, with little evidence that it carries over to unrelated, real-world thinking. Practise on the kind of decisions you actually face.
AI adds new traps
A 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in AI went with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more. A 2026 study in Science found leading chatbots affirm users far more than humans do — and that users prefer the agreeable version anyway.
Critical ignoring is now a core skill
Researchers argue that online, choosing what NOT to attend to matters as much as careful reading: self-nudging, lateral reading, and not feeding the trolls.
The Codex is a map, not a law
The famous 188-bias wheel arranges Wikipedia's list under four problems. It is a superb memory aid, but it mixes useful heuristics with errors, many entries overlap, and not all are equally well evidenced.

✦ The 22 Non-Negotiable Rules ✦

01 Correlation is NOT causation. Just because two things happen together doesn't mean one caused the other.
02 Strong opinions, loosely held. Be willing to change your mind when evidence says so.
03 "I don't know" is the most powerful sentence. It's the beginning of learning, not the end.
04 Seek to understand before seeking to be understood. Listen first, judge later.
05 The map is not the territory. Your model of reality is always an approximation.
06 Check the incentives. When confused about why something is happening, follow the money — but remember that a motive is not a proof.
07 Beware of false dichotomies. Most choices aren't either/or — look for the third option. The middle is not automatically right either.
08 Think in probabilities, not certainties. Nothing is 100% — assign confidence levels.
09 Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The bigger the claim, the higher the bar.
10 The burden of proof lies with whoever makes the claim — including when that person is you.
11 Anecdotes are not data. One story, however moving, is a sample of one.
12 Start with the base rate. Ask how often this happens in general before judging this case.
13 Judge the decision, not the outcome. Good bets sometimes lose; bad bets sometimes win.
14 Confidence is not evidence. Neither is fluency, repetition, popularity or a title.
15 Be sceptical, not cynical. Scepticism asks for evidence; cynicism refuses to look at it.
16 One standard for all. Test the claims you like exactly as hard as the claims you dislike.
17 Attack ideas, not people. A person you dislike can still be right.
18 You are the easiest person to fool. Knowing the names of biases does not make you immune to them.
19 When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Watch what gets gamed.
20 Update when the facts change. Changing your mind on evidence is a strength, not a defeat.
21 Verify in proportion to the stakes. The more it matters, the more you check.
22 Not everything deserves your attention. Choosing what to ignore is part of thinking well.
